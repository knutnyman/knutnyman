# ACA Subsidy Expiration & the One Big Beautiful Bill: Implications for Medtech Procedure Volumes

*Analysis as of May 2026*

---

## Executive Summary

Two converging policy shocks are reshaping the U.S. healthcare coverage landscape—and, with a lag, the procedure volumes that underpin medtech revenue:

1. **ACA enhanced subsidies expired January 1, 2026**, stripping roughly $2,000/year in premium support from ~22 million marketplace enrollees and pushing an estimated 5–7 million off coverage.
2. **The One Big Beautiful Bill (OBB)**, signed July 4, 2025, cuts $325.8 billion from Medicaid over ten years and imposes work requirements beginning January 2027, putting an additional 10–16 million Americans on a coverage-loss pathway by 2034.

Combined, the CBO projects **at least 14.2 million more uninsured Americans by 2034** relative to pre-OBB baselines. The transmission from coverage loss to procedure volume is not instantaneous—acuity-driven and cardiac procedures are insulated; elective musculoskeletal and bariatric volumes face material multi-year headwinds. Medtech companies with concentrated exposure to elective, Medicaid-heavy, or low-acuity procedure categories face the greatest earnings risk.

---

## 1. The Coverage Loss Stack

### 1.1 ACA Subsidy Expiration (Already in Effect)

The American Rescue Plan (2021) and Inflation Reduction Act (2022) enhanced premium tax credits were not extended by Congress. Beginning January 1, 2026:

| Metric | Data Point | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Prior ACA enrollment (2025) | 24.1 million | CMS |
| 2026 ACA enrollment | ~23 million (–1.4M) | CMS 2026 snapshot |
| Enrollees now uninsured | ~9% of prior enrollees (KFF survey) | CNBC/KFF, Mar 2026 |
| Projected total marketplace exits | 5–7.3 million | Urban Institute / Commonwealth Fund |
| Average premium increase for enrollees who stayed | ~26% | AJMC |
| Additional benchmark premium cost for 40-yr-old at $50K income | ~$2,000/year | CNBC |

Early-in-year enrollment data suggest the coverage loss may be tracking below the worst-case scenario (actual enrollment decline is ~1.4M vs. early fears of 4M+), but surveys show a significant share of remaining enrollees are financially stressed—cutting food and clothing spending to maintain premiums—which creates a secondary demand suppression effect on elective care even among the still-insured.

### 1.2 One Big Beautiful Bill — Medicaid Provisions

Key structural changes taking effect between now and 2027:

- **Work requirements**: Starting January 2027, most expansion-population Medicaid recipients must document 80 hrs/month of work, training, or volunteering. CBO projects 5+ million coverage losses from this provision alone by 2034.
- **Spending cut**: $325.8B in federal Medicaid funding cuts over 10 years.
- **Administrative burden**: New eligibility re-verification requirements will cause "paperwork churn"—people losing coverage due to administrative failures rather than actual ineligibility.
- **Early state rollouts**: Georgia (2023), Nebraska (May 2026), Montana (July 2026), Iowa (December 2026) are implementing work requirements ahead of the federal mandate.

### 1.3 Combined Coverage Loss Trajectory

```
                        Additional Uninsured vs. 2024 Baseline
  2026:  ~5–7M  (ACA subsidy expiration, already biting)
  2027:  ~8–11M (Medicaid work requirements begin)
  2034:  ~14–16M (fully phased OBB + sustained ACA non-extension)
```

Approximately **70% of Medicaid coverage losses are procedural in nature** (PR Newswire, 2025), meaning the bulk of lost lives map directly onto procedure-generating patient encounters.

## 2. Transmission Mechanism: Coverage Loss → Procedure Volume

Insurance coverage and surgical procedure volume are meaningfully correlated, though the relationship is non-linear and category-specific.

### 2.1 Quantitative Anchor

Academic research on ACA expansion (PMC) found approximately **4,659 additional elective surgeries performed nationally per 1 percentage point increase in insurance coverage**. Inverting this: a 1pp reduction in coverage reduces elective surgical volume by a similar magnitude. A 14-million-person coverage loss over the decade represents roughly a 4–5pp reduction in covered population—implying potential loss of 18,000–23,000 elective surgeries annually at steady state.

This is a floor, not a ceiling. Real-world effects are amplified by:
- **Underinsurance**: Survivors of premium shock who retained coverage but face higher deductibles ($162% increase in deductibles, 2009–2019) are deferring elective care.
- **Provider financial stress**: Hospitals facing $32.1B in lost revenue by end-2026 and $7.7B in additional uncompensated care will cut capacity, defer capital purchases, and reduce elective scheduling slots—depressing device pull-through even where patients nominally have coverage.
- **Job loss multiplier**: Commonwealth Fund projects 340,000 healthcare jobs lost in 2026 from subsidy expiration alone, further compressing institutional procedure capacity.

### 2.2 Leading Indicators Already Moving

| Provider Indicator | Data |
|---|---|
| HCA Healthcare Q1 2026 ACA admissions | Down ~15% YoY |
| HCA full-year 2026 EBITDA guidance impact | –$600M to –$900M |
| Hospital executives expecting negative procedure volume impact | 44% (BTIG survey) |
| U.S. provider revenue loss projected, full year 2026 | $32.1B |
| Uncompensated care demand increase | +$7.7B |

Critically, BTIG's March 2026 survey of hospital executives found that while the **majority had not yet seen medtech device purchasing impacted**, the 44% expecting future deterioration represents a leading signal given the typical 6–12 month lag between payer mix shifts and device budget decisions.

## 3. Medtech Segment Vulnerability Framework

Not all medtech is equally exposed. The key variables are: **(a) procedure electivity**, **(b) Medicaid/ACA payer mix of the patient population**, and **(c) procedure deferability** (can the patient wait months or years?).

### 3.1 High Vulnerability — Elective, Deferrable Procedures

**Orthopedics (Joints / Spine)**

- Total joint replacement (hip, knee) is substantially elective. Patients in pain can defer; uninsured patients nearly always do.
- ACA expansion (2014–2015) was directly associated with increased elective joint replacement volumes. The reversal will work symmetrically.
- Younger patients (18–64) in ACA/Medicaid expansion states are disproportionately affected—this is the prime demographic for musculoskeletal procedures in early-to-mid disease stages.
- *Affected companies*: Stryker (Mako robotics utilization), Zimmer Biomet, Smith+Nephew, Globus Medical.

**Bariatric / General Surgery Devices**

- Hospital executives in BTIG surveys specifically cited plastic/cosmetic and bariatric as the procedure categories expected to see the *least* growth in 2026. These are highly elective and heavily insurance-gated.
- *Affected companies*: Intuitive Surgical (da Vinci utilization in soft tissue), Becton Dickinson (surgical stapling), Medtronic (general surgery).

**Ophthalmology (Elective LASIK / Cataract)**

- Cataract surgery is partially covered by Medicare (so lower exposure), but premium IOLs and elective refractive procedures are consumer-discretionary and will compress in a financially stressed patient population.
- *Affected companies*: Alcon, Johnson & Johnson Vision.

### 3.2 Moderate Vulnerability — Semi-Elective, Mixed Urgency

**Spine**

- Degenerative spine disease spans a wide urgency spectrum. Cervical myelopathy is less deferrable; lumbar stenosis is more so. Expect volume softness at the elective end.
- *Affected companies*: Globus Medical, NuVasive (Nuvasive/Globus merged), Alphatec, Orthofix.

**Wound Care / Chronic Disease Management**

- Medicaid covers a high proportion of chronic wound patients (diabetic foot ulcers, pressure injuries). Coverage loss → delayed presentation → more severe disease at first encounter, but *fewer billable device encounters* in the interim.
- *Affected companies*: Acelity (3M Health), MiMedx, Integra LifeSciences.

**Diabetes Devices (CGM, Insulin Delivery)**

- Medicaid is a large payer for diabetes management devices. Work requirements and disenrollment will create coverage gaps for CGM refills and insulin pump supplies.
- *Affected companies*: Dexcom, Abbott (Libre), Insulet, Medtronic (diabetes).

### 3.3 Lower Vulnerability — Acuity-Driven, Less Deferrable

**Cardiac / Electrophysiology**

- Hospital executives rank cath lab and cardiac surgery as the procedure categories *most* expected to grow in 2026, even amid the ACA headwinds.
- Heart attacks, unstable arrhythmias, and structural heart disease have limited deferability—patients present to emergency departments regardless of insurance status (EMTALA requirements).
- However, **elective EP procedures** (AF ablation, ICD implants for primary prevention) have more discretion and could face softness among newly uninsured patients who defer diagnostic workups.
- *Relatively insulated*: Abbott (structural heart), Edwards Lifesciences (TAVR), Boston Scientific (EP), Medtronic (CRM emergent side).

**Neurology / Neurostimulation**

- Stroke intervention (thrombectomy) is emergent. Neuromodulation (SCS, DBS) is more elective and moderately exposed.
- *Mixed*: Penumbra (emergent neurovascular insulated), Abbott/Medtronic neuromod (moderate exposure).

**Endoscopy / GI**

- Cancer surveillance colonoscopy is preventive; the ACA required no-cost coverage for preventive colonoscopies, but coverage loss could delay cancer screening uptake among newly uninsured—creating a longer-term cancer burden, not a near-term volume boost.
- *Affected longer-term*: Olympus, Medtronic (GI).

## 4. Geographic Concentration Risk

Coverage loss is highly uneven geographically. States with large ACA marketplace enrollments and Medicaid expansion (particularly Sun Belt states that expanded late) face the steepest acute exposure:

**Highest-risk hospital markets (HFMA, 2025 analysis: 12 states with high financial risk from subsidy expiration)**:
- Florida (~3M+ ACA enrollees, no state-level backstop)
- Texas (large Medicaid non-expansion population, high uninsured baseline)
- Georgia, North Carolina, South Carolina (large rural ACA-dependent populations)

For medtech, this matters because **procedure concentration tracks geography**. Device companies with dominant positions in rural hospital systems, Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHCs), and safety-net hospitals in these states face outsized volume risk.

## 5. Timing and Phasing of Impact

```
Phase 1 (H1 2026 — NOW)
├── ACA subsidy expiration in effect
├── ~1.4M have dropped coverage (enrollment data) 
├── Additional 9% of prior enrollees uninsured (KFF survey)
├── HCA admissions already –15%; $150M Q1 EBITDA hit
└── Medtech: no confirmed device volume impact yet (BTIG)

Phase 2 (H2 2026 – 2027)
├── Hospital budget cycles begin reflecting payer mix shift
├── Capital equipment purchase deferrals begin (device companies feel lag)
├── Work requirement implementation begins (state-by-state)
├── Medicaid disenrollment ramps
└── Elective procedure deferrals accumulate

Phase 3 (2027–2030)
├── Full Medicaid work requirement enforcement nationwide
├── 8–11M additional uninsured vs. 2024 baseline
├── Hospital financial stress forces capacity reduction / closure
├── Chronic disease progression in uninsured creates ER-heavy (lower-margin) acuity mix
└── Medtech volumes measurably lower in elective categories; emergent volumes elevated

Phase 4 (2030–2034)
├── $325.8B cumulative Medicaid cuts fully absorbed
├── 14–16M additional uninsured vs. 2024 baseline (CBO combined estimate)
└── Structural long-term demand suppression in preventive + elective care
```

The critical insight on timing: **medtech companies will see the impact with a 6–18 month lag** behind the coverage events themselves, because hospital capital budgets (where most device contracts are set) are annual and update on a trailing basis. Q3/Q4 2026 earnings calls are the likely first clear visibility window.

## 6. Scenario Analysis

### Bear Case (Full Impact, No Policy Reversal)
- 7M ACA exits by end-2026; OBB fully implemented through 2027
- Hospital EBITDA pressure triggers 10–15% capital equipment budget cuts across elective procedure lines
- Elective orthopedic volumes down 5–8% by 2027 vs. 2024 peak
- Bariatric/general surgery device volumes down 8–12%
- Medtech companies with 30–40% U.S. revenue exposure to elective soft tissue/musculoskeletal see 200–400 bps revenue headwind by 2027

### Base Case (Partial Offset, Market Adaptation)
- Enrollment decline stabilizes around 1.5–2M (tracking current CMS data)
- Work requirements partially blocked by litigation or administrative delays (similar to Arkansas 2019 pattern)
- Hospitals adapt: convert some elective capacity to higher-acuity cardiac/structural work
- Elective orthopedic volumes flat to –3% by 2027; cardiac/EP volumes +3–5%
- Medtech: bifurcated — cardiac/structural heart outperforms; musculoskeletal/bariatric underperforms

### Bull Case (Policy Reversal or State Backstops)
- Congress reinstates partial enhanced subsidies in 2027 reconciliation
- Multiple states implement state-level premium assistance programs
- Courts block work requirements (as occurred in Arkansas under ACA)
- Coverage loss contained to 2–3M
- Procedure volumes recover; medtech volumes largely unaffected

**Current trajectory is tracking closer to base case** on the enrollment side, but the OBB Medicaid provisions have significantly longer fuses (work requirements Jan 2027) and are a more durable structural risk than the ACA subsidy lapse.

## 7. Investment / Strategic Implications

### Relative Positioning Within Medtech

| Category | Coverage Risk | Acuity Offset | Net Outlook |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural heart (TAVR, MitraClip) | Low | High | Positive |
| Cardiac rhythm management (ICD, CRT) — emergent | Low | High | Positive |
| Electrophysiology (AF ablation) — elective | Moderate | Low-moderate | Neutral |
| Neuro — stroke (thrombectomy) | Low | High | Positive |
| Neuro — stimulation (SCS, DBS) | Moderate | Low | Neutral–Negative |
| Orthopedics — joints | High | None | Negative |
| Spine — elective | High | Low | Negative |
| Bariatric / general surgery | High | None | Negative |
| Diabetes devices (CGM, pumps) | High (Medicaid) | Low | Negative |
| Wound care | High (Medicaid) | Low | Negative |
| Ophthalmology — premium IOL | Moderate | None | Neutral–Negative |

### Key Monitoring Metrics for Medtech Investors

1. **Hospital payer mix quarterly disclosures** (HCA, Tenet, CHS) — leading indicator for device pull-through
2. **ACA enrollment monthly CMS snapshots** — net enrollment vs. 2025 baseline
3. **State work requirement implementation dates and litigation outcomes**
4. **BTIG / MedTech Dive hospital executive survey** (bi-annual) — capital budget intentions
5. **Uncompensated care as % of net revenue** at major health systems — proxy for procedure volume pressure
6. **Device company commentary on procedure volume by category** in Q2–Q3 2026 earnings calls

### The Counterintuitive Dynamic: Emergent Volume Shift

As coverage loss advances chronic disease and delays prevention, medtech may see a **compositional shift** in procedure acuity rather than a pure volume decline. More patients presenting with advanced disease creates:
- More complex/higher-ASP cardiac interventions (vs. prevention-focused ablations)
- More emergent orthopedic fracture repair (vs. elective joint replacement)
- More late-stage oncology device needs (vs. early-detection-driven interventions)

This shift favors acute-care-oriented device categories but compresses the profitable, high-volume elective segment where medtech margins have historically been widest.

---

## 8. Key Risks to This Analysis

- **Enrollment data lag**: CMS snapshots are backward-looking; the full Q1 2026 coverage picture may not be clear until mid-2026.
- **Litigation**: OBB Medicaid provisions (particularly work requirements) face active legal challenges. Arkansas's 2019 work requirement was blocked by courts; similar dynamics are plausible.
- **State-level variation**: States with their own subsidy programs (California, Massachusetts, New York, Washington) will partly buffer federal coverage loss—device companies with greater Northeast/West Coast hospital concentration are less exposed.
- **Procedure backlog**: Post-COVID backlog in some categories may sustain volumes longer than coverage data alone would suggest.
- **Macro offset**: Strong employment and GDP could reduce the number of people actually losing coverage (most people would rather pay higher premiums than go uninsured if they can afford it).
