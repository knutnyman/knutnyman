Key Dates & Timeline
Chronological
April 1962
Trade Expansion Act — Section 232 Created
Enables the President to restrict imports on national security grounds via the Secretary of Commerce investigation process. Rarely invoked until 2018.
April 20, 2017
Commerce Dept Initiates Steel & Aluminum Investigations
Trump administration launches Section 232 national security investigations into steel and aluminum imports — the formal start of the modern tariff era.
SteelAluminum
March 8, 2018
Presidential Proclamations 9704 & 9705 Signed
Trump signs proclamations imposing 25% tariff on steel (Proc. 9705) and 10% tariff on aluminum (Proc. 9704) imports. Canada and Mexico initially excluded pending NAFTA renegotiation.
Steel 25%Aluminum 10%Policy
March 23, 2018
Tariffs Take Effect — MedTech Supply Chain Disrupted
25% steel and 10% aluminum tariffs go into force globally (except temporary exemptions for Canada, Mexico, EU, Australia, Argentina, Brazil, South Korea). MedTech manufacturers begin feeling cost pressure on stainless steel surgical instruments, implant materials, device housings, and diagnostic imaging equipment.
MedTech Impact
May–Oct 2018
BIS Opens Product Exclusion Request Process
Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) opens portal for companies to file product-specific exclusion requests. MedTech companies begin submitting requests for surgical-grade stainless steel, titanium alloys, and aluminum used in device manufacturing.
Exclusions
July 1, 2018
Canada & Mexico: Tariffs Imposed, Then Lifted
Steel/aluminum tariffs briefly applied to Canada and Mexico before exemptions restored. This back-and-forth created significant supply uncertainty for MedTech companies sourcing raw materials across North American borders.
Policy
May 20, 2019
Canada & Mexico USMCA Exemptions Made Permanent
Tariffs on Canadian and Mexican steel/aluminum lifted permanently as part of USMCA ratification. Major relief for US MedTech manufacturers relying on Canadian specialty steel and Mexican manufacturing operations.
AluminumSteel
October 2021
EU: Tariff Rate Quotas (TRQs) Replace Tariffs
US-EU agreement replaces steel/aluminum tariffs with tariff rate quotas for EU origin goods. Within-quota imports enter duty-free; above-quota subject to 25%/10%. Mixed impact for MedTech depending on volume of EU specialty steel sourcing.
March 2022
UK, Japan Granted TRQ Arrangements
Similar tariff rate quota frameworks established for UK and Japanese steel/aluminum. Relevant for companies sourcing precision steel from Japan or stainless from UK specialty mills.
February 10, 2025
Trump 2.0: New Proclamations — All Exemptions Revoked
President Trump signs new proclamations eliminating all prior exemptions, quota arrangements, and TRQs. Steel tariff maintained at 25%; aluminum tariff increased from 10% to 25%. Canada and Mexico lose USMCA exemptions. EU, UK, Japan TRQs terminated. This is the most significant escalation since 2018.

March 12, 2025
Expanded Tariffs Take Effect — MedTech Cost Crisis
25% steel + 25% aluminum tariffs now apply to ALL countries globally with no exceptions. Canadian and Mexican content — heavily used in MedTech supply chains — is now tariffed. Companies with North American manufacturing (Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, Medtronic) face immediate COGS increases. Industry estimates $2–4B annual impact across sector.

Advanced Medical Technology Association (AdvaMed) files formal comment letters with BIS requesting categorical exclusions for medical-grade stainless steel, surgical titanium alloys, and aluminum used in Class II/III devices. FDA and HHS also engage with USTR on humanitarian device carve-outs.

BIS Exclusion Decisions — Mixed Outcomes
BIS begins issuing decisions on 2025 exclusion requests. Medical device manufacturers receive partial relief: limited categorical exclusion for surgical-grade Type 316L stainless steel in Class III implants approved; broader aluminum exclusions for diagnostic imaging denied. Companies without exemptions seek alternate sourcing in India, Taiwan, and domestic US mills.

Multiple trading partners (EU, UK, Japan, Canada) pursuing bilateral deals to reinstate quota arrangements. No MedTech-specific carve-out achieved yet. BIS exclusion portal remains open. Watch for Commerce Dept policy reviews tied to trade negotiations.

MedTech Product Impact Matrix
By Device Category
🦴 Orthopedic Implants
CRITICAL
Hip/knee replacements, spinal implants, bone screws. Primary materials are cobalt-chrome and titanium (not Section 232 covered), but stainless steel implants and surgical instruments for procedures are heavily impacted.
Steel tariff exposure: HIGH (instruments) | Implant materials: LOW (Ti/CoCr)
🔬 Surgical Instruments
CRITICAL
Forceps, scissors, retractors, clamps — almost universally Type 316L stainless steel. Largely sourced from Germany (Tuttlingen cluster), Japan, and Pakistan. All now face 25% tariff with no exemption pathway.
Tariff exposure: 25% on nearly all imported units
📡 Diagnostic Imaging (MRI/CT)
HIGH
MRI gantries, CT frames, X-ray tables use substantial steel and aluminum. GE Healthcare, Siemens Healthineers, Philips — all manufacture globally. Large capital equipment with long replacement cycles means price increases hard to pass through quickly.
Steel + aluminum exposure: $50–300K per system in additional tariff cost
🤖 Surgical Robots
HIGH
Intuitive Surgical's da Vinci, Stryker Mako, Zimmer Biomet ROSA — precision aluminum and steel structural components. Systems cost $1–2M+ and contain significant aluminum alloy content for weight/precision.
Aluminum (25%) is primary exposure; significant BOM cost impact
💉 Infusion & IV Devices
MEDIUM
Infusion pumps, IV poles, administration sets — steel and aluminum components in pumps and stands. Lower tariff exposure as percentage of total COGS vs. capital equipment, but high volume amplifies absolute dollar impact.
Moderate per-unit impact at scale
🫀 Cardiac Rhythm Devices
MEDIUM
Pacemakers, ICDs, CRT devices — titanium cases (not Section 232), but manufacturing equipment and tooling have steel exposure. Medtronic, Abbott, Boston Scientific manufacturing in Ireland, US, Dominican Republic each face different supply chain tariff profiles.
Indirect exposure via manufacturing tooling and equipment
🏥 Hospital Furniture & Beds
HIGH
Hospital beds, operating tables, procedure carts, stainless steel sinks — high steel content, often sourced from Canada, EU, or China. Stryker Medical Beds segment and Hill-Rom (Baxter) directly exposed.
25% tariff on substantial steel content per unit
🫁 Respiratory & Ventilators
MEDIUM
Ventilators, CPAP/BiPAP devices — aluminum housings and internal components. Significant post-COVID domestic manufacturing investment reduced import dependency, but specialized aluminum components still sourced internationally.
Aluminum (25%) is primary material exposure

Exclusion & Mitigation Pathways
BIS Process & Alternatives
🛡️ BIS Product Exclusion Requests (Primary Route)
▼
  • Filed through BIS portal (bis.doc.gov) — company-specific or industry-wide requests
  • Must demonstrate: (1) product unavailable domestically in sufficient quantity/quality, OR (2) specific national security / humanitarian need
  • Medical device manufacturers should cite FDA-mandated material specifications (e.g., ASTM F138 for 316L surgical steel) as basis for domestic unavailability
  • Requests can be opposed by US domestic producers (Nucor, US Steel, Century Aluminum)
  • Processing time: 90–270+ days; approvals are product/HTS-code specific, not blanket
  • Approved exclusions are company-specific (other companies must file separately) and typically 1-year duration
  • Key tip: Include FDA 510(k) or PMA documentation, ASTM/ISO material standards citations, and volume data in the request
🏭 Domestic Sourcing Alternatives
▼
  • Carpenter Technology (CRS) — Reading, PA; surgical-grade stainless and specialty alloys; premium pricing but tariff-free
  • Allegheny Technologies (ATI) — Pittsburgh, PA; specialty stainless and titanium; strong MedTech customer base
  • Haynes International — Kokomo, IN; high-performance alloys for implants
  • Universal Forest Products / Olympic Steel — stainless service centers for sheet/coil
  • Domestic capacity constrained — lead times extended 3–6 months in 2025; price premiums of 10–20% over import + tariff may still apply
  • For aluminum: Novelis (Atlanta, GA), Arconic — both produce medical-grade aluminum alloys
🌏 Supply Chain Diversification
▼
  • India — Growing stainless steel sector; Jindal Stainless and Apar Industries export surgical-grade steel; still faces 25% tariff but competitive base price
  • Taiwan — Precision steel and aluminum machining for medical devices; 25% tariff applies but high quality
  • South Korea — Steel quota arrangement reduces exposure; POSCO is a major supplier of specialty grades
  • Australia — Full exemption maintained but limited specialty medical steel capacity
  • Note: Supply chain shifting takes 12–24 months minimum due to qualification requirements for regulated medical device materials
💰 Financial / Customs Mitigation
▼
  • Foreign Trade Zones (FTZs) — Defer or potentially reduce tariff exposure; applicable if materials are further manufactured in the US
  • Bonded Warehouses — Defer tariff payment; useful for working capital but doesn't reduce liability
  • Duty Drawback — If importing material and then exporting finished devices, can recover ~99% of tariffs paid; complex but valuable for export-heavy companies
  • First Sale Valuation — Use factory price (not importer price) as customs value basis — reduces tariff base
  • Contract renegotiation — Push tariff burden to overseas suppliers; success depends on supplier leverage
  • Price pass-through — Limited in MedTech due to GPO contracts and hospital budget pressure; CMS reimbursement lag
⚖️ Legal Challenges
▼
  • Multiple importers have challenged Section 232 tariffs in the Court of International Trade (CIT) — largely unsuccessful; courts have upheld Presidential authority
  • CAFC (Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit) has consistently upheld broad Presidential discretion under Section 232
  • Legislative route: Congress could pass medical device tariff exemption legislation — AdvaMed actively lobbying; bipartisan support exists but passage uncertain
  • WTO disputes ongoing — EU, Canada, others filed complaints; rulings largely favor complainants but US has blocked Appellate Body appointments, limiting enforcement

Action Items & Checklist
For MedTech companies
  • ☐
    File BIS exclusion requests for all affected HTS codes URGENT
    Deadline: rolling — but earlier submissions get earlier decisions. Include ASTM material specs, volume data, and FDA device classification documentation.
  • ☐
    Conduct full BOM tariff audit across all imported materials URGENT
    Map every steel and aluminum input by HTS code, country of origin, and annual import volume. Quantify the total 232 tariff exposure precisely.
  • ☐
    Review and update supplier contracts for tariff allocation clauses HIGH
    Many older contracts are silent on tariff responsibility. Renegotiate to include tariff pass-through provisions or price adjustment mechanisms tied to Section 232 rates.
  • ☐
    Qualify domestic US steel/aluminum suppliers (Carpenter Tech, ATI, Novelis) HIGH
    Requires material qualification testing under FDA CFR Part 820 / ISO 13485. Start now — 12–18 month qualification timelines. Request material certs and begin testing.
  • ☐
    Evaluate Foreign Trade Zone (FTZ) status for manufacturing facilities HIGH
    If importing raw materials for further manufacture and export, FTZ status + duty drawback can recover significant tariff costs. Engage customs counsel.
  • ☐
    Join AdvaMed Section 232 working group and co-sign industry comment letters MEDIUM
    Collective industry voice is most effective for BIS exclusion decisions and Congressional action. Coordinate with AdvaMed for coordinated filings and lobbying.
  • ☐
    Model USMCA renegotiation scenarios (Q3 2026 watch date) MEDIUM
    Restoration of Canada/Mexico exemptions is the single largest potential relief event. Model financial impact and have sourcing strategy ready to adapt quickly.
  • ☐
    Disclose Section 232 exposure in 10-K / annual report risk factors MEDIUM
    SEC expects material tariff exposure disclosure. Quantify impact where possible. Consult securities counsel on materiality thresholds.
  • ☐
    Explore South Korean sourcing for steel inputs (quota arrangement maintained) MEDIUM
    South Korea retains a quota arrangement for steel — within quota, rate is effectively 0%. POSCO and POSCO International supply surgical-grade stainless. Investigate volume availability.
